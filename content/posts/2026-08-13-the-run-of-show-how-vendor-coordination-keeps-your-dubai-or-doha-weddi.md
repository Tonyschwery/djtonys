Every wedding I've played, from an intimate garden ceremony in Doha to a full Lebanese reception with a live band, DJ, and zaffe, has one thing in common. The nights that flow are the nights where someone wrote a run-of-show and every vendor actually read it.

The run-of-show is the least glamorous document at any wedding and the most important one. It's a minute-by-minute schedule that tells the photographer when the cake cutting happens, tells the caterer when to start plating, tells the MC when to call the couple in, and tells me exactly when to bring the energy up or bring it down. Without it, even the best individual vendors end up guessing what's next. With it, the whole event feels effortless — which is the goal.

## What a run-of-show actually looks like

A good run-of-show isn't a vague outline. It's specific, timed, and shared with every vendor in advance — not discovered on the day.

A typical Gulf wedding version might include:

- **Guest arrival and cocktail hour** — start time, background music style, duration
- **Grand entrance** — who walks first, what track plays, how long the walk takes
- **Zaffe or first dance** — exact cue, whether live percussion hands off to the DJ or the two run together
- **Speeches and toasts** — number of speakers, rough length, mic handoff plan
- **Dinner service** — when plates go out, when the DJ drops to background level, when the room comes back up
- **Cake cutting, sword ceremony, or family traditions** — timing and who signals the DJ
- **Open dance floor** — start time, how long before the first big set change, planned closing time

Every line has a time next to it, and every vendor gets the same version. That last part matters more than people think. I've been on wedding groups where the photographer had one timeline, the venue had another, and the DJ had a third — all slightly different. Small gaps like that are where nights start running behind.

## Why the DJ needs to be in this conversation early

A lot of couples build their run-of-show with the planner and venue, then send it to the DJ as a finished document a week before the wedding. That works fine if the timeline is realistic. It doesn't work if it isn't — and DJs often catch the problems nobody else does, because we're the ones reading the room in real time.

A few examples I flag regularly:

1. **Fifteen minutes for speeches always becomes thirty.** I build a buffer into the music plan so the energy doesn't die waiting on a fourth toast nobody scheduled.
2. **Dinner and dancing back-to-back kills momentum.** Guests need a beat between courses ending and the floor opening, even if it's only ten minutes of transitional music.
3. **Sunset photos and first dance often compete for the same window.** In Doha and Dubai particularly, couples want that golden-hour shot, and it can quietly eat into time planned for the entrance or first dance if nobody accounts for it.

None of this is about taking over the timeline. It's about being one more set of experienced eyes on it, because I've watched hundreds of these nights unfold in real time and know where they tend to slip.

## How this changes for mixed Arabic and Western receptions

Weddings that combine a zaffe, Arabic dance sets, and a Western-style open floor need a tighter run-of-show than a single-tradition event, simply because there are more moving parts and more vendors involved — a live percussion section, an MC, sometimes a second band, and the DJ all sharing the same clock. I've written before about [programming the music for that kind of night](/), but the scheduling side deserves its own attention. The safest approach is to build the timeline around the traditions first, then fit the dinner service and speeches around them, rather than the other way around. Traditions have less flexibility than a caterer's plating schedule does.

## What to ask your planner or DJ before the day

If you're planning a wedding in Doha, Dubai, Abu Dhabi, or Beirut, a few questions early on will save you a stressful evening later:

- Is there a single, shared run-of-show every vendor has confirmed receiving?
- Who has the authority to adjust timing live if something runs long — the planner, the MC, or the DJ?
- Has a buffer been built in around speeches and family traditions?
- Does the DJ know the exact cue points for zaffe, entrance, and cake cutting, or just approximate times?
- Is there a sound check scheduled before guests arrive, so gear and mic handoffs are tested, not assumed?

That last one sounds basic, but it's the one most often skipped when a venue is being used for back-to-back events in one day, which is common in busy Gulf hotel ballrooms during peak wedding season.

## Why this matters more in the Gulf specifically

Weddings here often run longer and involve more vendors than a typical Western reception — a live band alongside a DJ, a zaffe procession, multiple outfit changes, sometimes a second reception the following night. More vendors means more places for the schedule to drift. A tight run-of-show, agreed by everyone in advance, is what keeps a five-hour night feeling like it moves in twenty minutes.

It's also why I'd rather be looped into the planning conversation early than handed a document the week of. Twenty years of reading rooms across Doha, Dubai, Beirut, and beyond means I usually know within the first hour of a wedding whether the timeline was built with the music in mind — and it shows in how the floor responds.
