Every wedding I play, no matter how modern the rest of the set is, has one moment where a disco or 80s classic comes in and the whole room changes. Aunties who were sitting all night are suddenly on the floor. Grooms who swore they don't dance are singing along. It happens in Beirut, it happens in Doha, it happens on a yacht in the Gulf. Disco never actually left. It just waits for the right moment.

## Why does disco still work on a modern dance floor?

Disco was built for exactly one job: keeping a room moving for hours without a break. The genre came out of clubs like Studio 54 in New York in the mid-to-late 1970s, where DJs strung tracks together at a steady tempo so the energy never dropped. That engineering — [the four-on-the-floor kick, the tempo range, the string arrangements built for tension and release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco) — is the direct ancestor of house music and most of what still fills clubs today. When I drop a disco record in 2026, I'm not playing nostalgia. I'm playing a track that was already perfectly built for the job.

The 80s took that foundation and pushed it further — Italo disco, early house, synth-driven pop — and that's the era I probably get the most requests for, especially from a Lebanese or Gulf crowd who grew up on it through their parents or through Arabic-language covers and remixes that borrowed those same basslines.

## How do you fit a disco classic into a modern set without killing the energy?

This is the part that takes 20 years to get comfortable with. A classic doesn't work if you just drop it in cold. It works when the room is already primed for it.

- **Read the BPM before you commit.** Most disco sits between 110-125 BPM, which is slower than most current club tracks. Coming down from a faster set needs a transition track, not a hard cut.
- **Use it as a release, not an opener.** The best moment for a disco classic is right after you've built tension with something modern — the crowd needs somewhere to exhale to.
- **Match the key, not just the mood.** Harmonic mixing works on old records exactly the same way it works on new ones. A well-chosen classic in the right key slides in instead of jarring the room.
- **Know your edits.** A lot of the best disco and 80s material now exists as extended remixes or edits built specifically for DJs — longer intros, cleaner outros, no dead air. That's often the difference between a track landing and a track clearing the floor.
- **Watch the room, not the plan.** If a classic gets a bigger reaction than you expected, let it breathe. Don't rush the next transition just because it wasn't in the plan.

## What genres actually count as "classics" in this region?

In Beirut, Doha, Dubai and across the Gulf, "classics" covers more ground than most people expect. It's disco, obviously, but also:

- **Italo disco and early Euro-pop** — huge with a crowd that grew up on European radio and cassette culture through the 80s and 90s.
- **Motown and soul** — still one of the most reliable ways to get an older generation onto the floor at a wedding.
- **90s house and Eurodance** — the bridge generation between disco-era production and today's club sound, and often the sweet spot for a mixed-age Lebanese wedding crowd.
- **Arabic classics and their Western-influenced remixes** — tracks from the 70s through 90s that borrowed disco and funk arrangements and are still instantly recognizable to a Levantine audience.

A good set for this region doesn't pick one of these lanes. It moves between them depending on who's in the room, and that's really the craft — knowing which classic fits which moment, for which crowd.
