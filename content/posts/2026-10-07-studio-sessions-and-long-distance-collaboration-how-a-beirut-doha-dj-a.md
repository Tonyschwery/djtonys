People assume a record gets made in one room, with one engineer, over one long night. That used to be closer to the truth. It isn't anymore. A track released today might start as an idea on a laptop in Mar Mikhael, get its drums rebuilt in a studio in Doha, and come back with a vocal recorded somewhere in Europe — all without anyone ever sitting in the same room.

I get asked about this a lot, usually by people who book me for a wedding or a club night and then notice the production credits on a release and want to know how that side of the work actually happens. So let's go through it properly.

## How does a DJ actually collaborate on a track with someone overseas?

The honest answer: the same way everyone else does now — stems, cloud folders, and a lot of patience. A collaboration usually starts one of two ways. Either someone sends a rough idea — a loop, a bassline, a vocal take — or two producers get on a call first and agree on a direction: the key, the rough BPM, the mood they're chasing. From there, the file gets passed back and forth as **stems** rather than a finished mix, so each person can rework their part without touching anyone else's.

Working across time zones between Beirut and the Gulf actually helps more than people expect. Qatar and the UAE sit a couple of hours ahead of Lebanon, so a stem sent at the end of a studio session in Beirut is often waiting, ready to react to, by the next morning in Doha. It turns into a relay rather than a conversation, and that's fine. Dance music has never needed real-time collaboration. It needs patience and a shared sense of where the track is going.

### What tools make this possible?

Nothing exotic. The same gear most working producers use:

- **A DAW that exports stems cleanly** — Ableton Live and Logic are the two I see most among people I work with.
- **Cloud storage or dedicated collaboration platforms** for passing large session files without losing quality.
- **Reference tracks** — everyone sends two or three records that describe the energy they're chasing, so nobody's guessing at the brief.
- **A shared sense of the Camelot wheel and BPM range** before anyone commits time, so the pieces actually fit together later.
- **Honest feedback loops** — a collaborator who only says "sounds great" is more trouble than one who tells you the drop isn't landing.

## Why do labels still matter when anyone can release music themselves?

You can put a track out yourself in an afternoon now. Plenty of people do. But a label still does something a bedroom release can't: it puts the record in front of an audience that already trusts that label's taste, and it puts the production itself under a second set of ears before it goes out. Working with **Inkognito Records, Sensual Ibiza, and Blackhole Records** over the years has meant exactly that — someone on the other end who knows the genre's history asking whether the arrangement earns its runtime, whether the low end is going to translate on a real club system, whether the track still has somewhere to go at the three-minute mark.

That last point matters more than people think. A track built for streaming and a track built to be mixed into a two-hour club set are not the same object. A DJ-facing release needs long intros and outros, a clean breakdown to mix through, and a low end that doesn't fight whatever's playing before or after it. A label that releases dance music for clubs, not just for playlists, keeps that discipline alive.

## Does touring change how you write music?

Constantly. Writing in a hotel room after a gig in Doha is a different headspace than writing at home in Beirut with no deadline. On the road, ideas come fast and rough — a four-bar loop, a vocal chop, something recorded on headphones at 3am because the energy of the night is still in your hands. Those fragments usually need months of distance before they become anything finished. The slower sessions, the ones where a track actually gets built from the ground up, happen at home, where there's time to second-guess a bassline for a week and come back to it with fresh ears.

Playing out constantly is also where the research happens. Twenty-five years of reading rooms across Lebanon and the Gulf tells you things no studio session can. You learn which rhythms get Beirut moving that don't translate to a Gulf crowd, and the other way around. That knowledge ends up in the productions eventually, whether it's a Khaleeji-influenced percussion loop or a disco bassline that's been road-tested on a few hundred dance floors first.

## What does this mean if you're booking for an event, not a studio session?

More than people expect. A DJ who also produces brings things to a wedding or a club night that a pure selector can't:

1. **Custom edits.** A radio version rarely has the right length or arrangement for a key moment of the night — a first dance, an entrance, a zaffe transition. Production experience means building a clean edit that fits the moment exactly.
2. **A deeper ear for programming.** Understanding how a track is built — its arrangement, its energy points, where the drop actually sits — makes it easier to read when a record will work on a given crowd, not just whether it's currently popular.
3. **Original material in the set.** Occasionally weaving in unreleased or original productions gives a set something a purely curated playlist can't offer.

Studio work and live bookings feed each other. The sets shape the records, and the records sharpen the sets.
