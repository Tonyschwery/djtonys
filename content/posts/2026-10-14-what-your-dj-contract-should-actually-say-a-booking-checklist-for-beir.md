Most people booking a DJ for a wedding in Faqra or a private party in Mar Mikhael never read the contract closely. They look at the price, maybe listen to a mix, and sign. Then something goes wrong — the DJ arrives with the wrong gear, the set list doesn't match what was discussed, or nobody agreed on who pays if the event runs two hours over. A proper contract prevents almost all of that. Here's what actually belongs in one, and why.

## Why a verbal agreement isn't enough

In Lebanon, a lot of bookings still happen over WhatsApp. A voice note, a deposit transfer, a date in the calendar — and that's it. It works most of the time, right up until it doesn't. Weddings in particular have too many moving parts: a late ceremony, a power issue at the venue, a family member who insists on an extra hour of dabke. **A written contract turns assumptions into agreements**, and that protects both sides, not just the client.

This matters more, not less, once you're booking across borders. A Beirut-based DJ playing a wedding in Doha, or a Gulf client flying someone in for a private event, needs everything on paper simply because there's no easy way to sort out a misunderstanding in person after the fact.

## What should be in the contract

A solid DJ agreement, whether it's for a wedding in Faqra or a club night in Mar Mikhael, should cover:

1. **Date, times, and location** — including load-in time, the actual performance window, and what happens if the event runs long. Overtime should have a clear rate, agreed in advance, not negotiated at midnight.
2. **Scope of services** — DJ only, DJ plus MC duties, DJ plus live VJ visuals, or DJ plus a live musician. If visuals or a saxophonist are part of the night, say so explicitly.
3. **Equipment and technical requirements** — who provides the sound system and lighting, what the DJ brings (CDJs, mixer, laptop running Rekordbox or Serato), and what the venue or event company is responsible for. This is where a technical rider usually attaches as an addendum.
4. **Music preferences and restrictions** — genres to emphasize, songs to avoid, and any language mix (Arabic, French, English) the family wants. This is also where you note anything culturally sensitive, so there's no surprise on the night.
5. **Payment terms** — deposit amount, balance due date, and what currency. In Lebanon and the Gulf, currency and payment method should be spelled out clearly given how often this trips people up.
6. **Cancellation and rescheduling policy** — what happens if the client cancels, what happens if the DJ can't make it due to illness or force majeure, and whether a substitute DJ is acceptable.
7. **Power and backup requirements** — especially relevant in Lebanon, where outdoor venues and generator backup need to be confirmed ahead of time, not discovered mid-set.
8. **Liability and insurance** — who's responsible for gear damage, and whether the venue carries its own event insurance.

That list looks long, but most of it fits on two pages. The goal isn't paperwork for its own sake. It's making sure nobody is guessing on the day.

## What a contract can't fix

A contract won't make a DJ read a room well, and it won't replace a proper conversation before the event. I still sit down with every couple or event planner and go through the night in detail — who's walking in, what the first dance is, when the dabke line starts, when the energy should peak. None of that belongs in legal language. It belongs in a planning conversation, usually a few weeks out, where we build the actual run of show together.

Where the contract does help is in locking in the logistics so that conversation can focus on the music instead of worrying about whether the sound system will be ready on time.

## Questions worth asking before you sign

- Does the quote include sound and lighting, or just the DJ?
- Is there a backup plan if there's a power cut or equipment failure?
- What's the deposit, and is it refundable under any circumstances?
- Will the DJ take requests on the night, and how are those handled?
- Is there a written timeline for the event, or just a start and end time?

If a DJ or agency can't answer these clearly before you book, that's useful information on its own.

## Music prep is part of the agreement too

A good contract should also reflect how seriously the DJ takes preparation. Building a wedding or club set isn't just picking songs on the day — it's having clean, properly mastered versions of key tracks ready in advance, organized by key and BPM so transitions are smooth instead of jarring. Some DJs source tracks from record pools, others maintain personal archives built over decades, and some lean on curated libraries like [topdjcrates.com](https://topdjcrates.com) for DJ-ready WAV packs when they need a specific edit fast. Whatever the source, it should show up as part of the preparation the contract is paying for, not an afterthought.
