Every Lebanese family has someone who couldn't make it back. A brother in Montreal, a cousin in Lagos, an aunt in Sydney who hasn't seen Beirut since before the airport reopened after a renovation most guests don't even remember. For years the fix was a shaky phone held up by a bridesmaid. Now it's a proper feed — a dedicated camera, a clean audio line, sometimes a second screen at the venue itself so the room can wave back.

I get asked about this more each wedding season, in Beirut ballrooms and in Faqra chalets alike. Families want the zaffe, the first dance, and the cake cutting to land properly on a laptop in another time zone, not as a blurry rectangle with crowd noise drowning out the vows. That's a fair ask. It's also a request that changes how I build the night, not just how someone points a camera.

## Why Lebanese Weddings Are Going Live

Lebanon's diaspora is enormous relative to the population still living here — most estimates put the number of people of Lebanese descent abroad well above the number living in the country itself. That's not new. What's new is the infrastructure. Fast enough internet at most venues, affordable streaming platforms, and a generation of guests who are used to watching big moments on a screen because they've been doing it for every other milestone — graduations, gender reveals, funerals, too.

So the livestream isn't a gimmick anymore. It's closer to a standard request, especially for families with a strong overseas presence, which in Lebanon is most of them.

## What Changes When There's a Camera on the Night

A DJ's job at a wedding is already about reading a room and building an **energy curve**. Add a stream and you're building two experiences at once — one for the two hundred people dancing in front of you, and one for the people watching on a screen who can't feel the bass in their chest or smell the arak on the table next to them.

A few things shift:

1. **The audio feed has to be separated from the room sound.** What the dance floor hears through the main PA and what goes down the stream line are not the same mix. Room sound needs headroom and bass for the people physically there. The stream feed needs a cleaner, more controlled level so it doesn't clip or distort on someone's laptop speaker in Toronto.
2. **Timing announcements matter more.** If I'm about to bring in the bride and groom, or call the zaffe, that needs a verbal or visual cue that works for both audiences — loud enough to cut the room, clear enough that the stream audio doesn't just catch a wall of noise with no context.
3. **Song licensing gets a second look.** A private party playing a track in a tent is one thing. A stream broadcasting that same track to a public link is a different conversation, and some platforms will mute or flag audio that trips their copyright detection. It's worth knowing this before the big entrance gets cut off mid-song.
4. **Lighting needs to work for a camera, not just a room.** What looks great to the eye on the dance floor — deep blues, fast strobes — can wash out or strobe badly on a phone camera sensor. A good production team adjusts for both.

## The Technical Split: Room Sound vs. Stream Feed

This is the part most families don't think about until the week of the wedding. A single microphone or a single output from the DJ booth isn't enough once you're streaming. You generally want:

- A dedicated line-level feed from the mixer to the streaming encoder, separate from the amplified room sound
- A camera operator or fixed camera with its own audio pickup for ambient reaction — laughter, applause, the actual texture of the room
- A stable internet connection at the venue, tested well before guests arrive, not assumed because the Wi-Fi password is on a card at reception
- A backup plan — mobile hotspot, second device — because Faqra's signal in November is not always what Beirut's is in a hotel ballroom

This is exactly the kind of detail that belongs in a pre-wedding planning call with your DJ and your videographer together, not worked out separately and bolted on at the last minute.

## Programming for People Who Are Watching, Not Dancing

Music choices shift slightly too. A set built purely for a Beirut dance floor leans hard into energy peaks — Arabic pop into dabke into a Western throwback, back and forth, chasing the room's mood. That still works for the stream. But I'll often hold certain intimate moments — the couple's first dance, a parent dance, a dedication — a little longer and a little cleaner on the audio side, because those are the clips that get downloaded, replayed, and kept. They deserve a mix that sounds good on a phone speaker ten years from now, not just one that filled the room for three minutes in the moment.

## What to Ask Before You Add a Livestream

If you're planning a wedding in Beirut, Faqra, or anywhere in the Gulf and want the ceremony or reception streamed home:

- Ask your DJ whether they can provide a separate stream-ready audio feed, or whether that's the videographer's responsibility
- Confirm internet reliability at the venue weeks in advance, not the day before
- Decide who is hosting the stream — a private link to family, or something more public — since that affects music licensing and how loud you can safely run certain tracks
- Walk through the timeline together so the camera operator knows when the big moments are coming, the same way the DJ does

None of this needs to be complicated. It just needs a team that's talked to each other before the night starts.
