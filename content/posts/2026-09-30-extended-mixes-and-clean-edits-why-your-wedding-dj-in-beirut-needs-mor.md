Every couple has a song. The problem is that the version they love — the one from the radio, the wedding video, the Spotify playlist — usually wasn't built to be mixed into anything. It starts cold, ends cold, and has a three-second silence before the drop that will stop a dance floor dead. That gap between "the song you know" and "the version a DJ can actually play" is where edits come in, and it's one of the least talked-about parts of what makes a night in **Beirut, Faqra, or Mar Mikhael** feel seamless instead of stitched together.

## What's actually wrong with the radio version?

Radio edits are built for one job: get a song on air in under four minutes with a clean fade at the end. That's it. Nobody making that edit was thinking about:

- **Mixing in and out** without a jarring cut
- **Intro length** long enough to beatmatch or blend from the previous track
- **Explicit content** swapped for something you can play in front of grandparents
- **A build that matches the room's energy**, not the artist's original arrangement

For a club night in **Mar Mikhael**, that might mean stretching a track's intro so it locks into the BPM of whatever came before it. For a **Beirut wedding**, it might mean cutting a verse that runs long so a first dance doesn't drag past the moment the couple actually wants to stop dancing and sit down.

## Where extended mixes come from

Most of what gets called an "extended mix" falls into a few categories:

1. **Official 12-inch or club versions** — the original extended cut a label released for DJs, often longer and with a proper breakdown before the drop.
2. **Bootleg or fan edits** — reworked by producers and edit-makers, usually to fix pacing, extend a build, or add a cleaner intro/outro for mixing.
3. **DJ-made edits** — built in the booth or beforehand in a program like Rekordbox or Serato, chopping and rearranging a track's own stems to serve a specific set.

A well-organized DJ shows up with all three types on hand, tagged and ready, not scrambling through Spotify search results mid-set. This is part of why library work matters as much as the actual mixing — a track without a usable intro is a track you can't drop cleanly when the room needs it. When I'm building out a fresh batch of edits for the crate, I'll sometimes pull curated, DJ-ready packs from a resource like [Top DJ Crates](https://topdjcrates.com) rather than hunting for one usable version across a dozen different files — it saves hours that should be going into actually reading the room instead.

## Why this matters more at a wedding than a club night

A club crowd is forgiving. They're already dancing, already warm, and a slightly rough transition won't break the night. A wedding is the opposite — every transition is watched, and half the room is filming. That's where clean edits earn their keep:

- **The entrance song** needs a clear, unmistakable start — no eight bars of ambient intro while 200 people wait to see the couple walk in.
- **The first dance** often needs to be trimmed to the length the couple actually rehearsed to, not the full album version.
- **The zaffe-to-DJ handoff** needs a track that can be brought in under the live percussion without stepping on it.
- **The last song of the night** needs an ending that actually ends — no long fade that leaves 40 people standing around wondering if it's over.

None of that is about picking better songs. It's about having the right version of the songs already chosen.

## Programming for a mixed Arabic and Western set

This is where edits do the most work in Lebanon specifically. A wedding in **Faqra** or a rooftop night in **Beirut** will often move between an Arabic classic, a 90s throwback, and a current house track inside a single ten-minute stretch. The BPMs don't naturally line up, and neither does the key. An edit that trims an intro to a clean four or eight bars, or extends a breakdown by a few seconds, is often the only thing that makes that jump feel intentional instead of abrupt. It's a small technical detail, but it's the difference between a set that flows and one that feels like a playlist on shuffle.

## Building your own edit library without losing your mind

For DJs starting to build this out, a few habits keep it manageable:

- **Tag everything by intro length and outro type**, not just genre and BPM. You want to know at a glance which tracks mix easily.
- **Keep a "safe for weddings" folder** separate from your club edits — clean versions only, no surprises live in front of family.
- **Re-edit your absolute go-to tracks yourself.** If you play a song every week, you'll know exactly where it drags and where it needs trimming.
- **Back up in more than one place.** An edit you spent an hour building for one specific song is not something you want to lose the week of a big booking.
