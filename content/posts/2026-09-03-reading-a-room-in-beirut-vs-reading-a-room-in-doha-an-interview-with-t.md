Twenty-five years behind the decks teaches you a lot of things — key matching, gear, how to fix a dead channel five minutes before doors open. But the skill that actually decides whether a night works is one nobody can teach from a manual: reading the room. We sat down with Tony Schwery to talk about how that instinct has been shaped by playing everywhere from Beirut's bars to Doha's lounges, and why the read changes completely depending on where — and who — you're playing for.

## How Do You Actually "Read a Room" as a DJ?

**"It's not one thing, it's ten small things happening at once."** Tony starts here every time someone asks. "You're watching how full the floor is, but also how people are standing when it's not full. Are they facing the booth? Are they talking with their backs to me? Are drinks moving fast or slow? Is anyone filming? All of that tells you where the energy actually is, not where you assume it is."

He's quick to point out that BPM and genre are the easy part. "Anyone with Rekordbox can build a set that flows on paper. The job is adjusting that set in real time because the room told you something the plan didn't account for."

## Is Reading a Beirut Crowd Different From Reading a Doha Crowd?

"Completely different, and that's what keeps this job interesting after this many years," Tony says. "In **Mar Mikhael**, you're often in a smaller room, close to the crowd, and Lebanese crowds are vocal — they'll tell you what they want, sometimes literally shouting it. That energy is a gift if you know how to use it without letting the request table run your set."

Doha, he says, is a slower build. "Gulf crowds, especially in lounge and hotel settings, warm up on trust. They need to feel the DJ knows what they're doing before they commit to the floor. You earn that first, usually in the first twenty minutes, before you can push tempo or take risks with the selection."

## What About Weddings in Faqra — Is the Read Even Different Again?

"Weddings are their own category everywhere, but **Faqra** specifically has its own rhythm," he explains. "You've got families across generations in one room, mountain acoustics that behave differently than a club, and a night structured around specific cultural moments — the zaffe, the first dance, the cake. The read there isn't just 'is the floor full,' it's 'am I honoring the structure the family expects while still building energy toward the peak of the night.'"

He adds that outdoor mountain venues change things physically too. "Sound travels differently outside, cooler air holds bass less than a packed indoor room. You're reading the room and the room's acoustics at the same time."

## Can You Give an Example of a Moment You Had to Change Course Mid-Set?

Tony laughs at this one. "Constantly. But the clearest example is always weddings — you'll have a floor going hard on Arabic pop, and then you sense the older guests starting to drift toward the exits or the bar. That's your signal. You don't kill the energy, you bridge it — bring in something familiar across generations, a 90s classic everyone in the room grew up on, and you buy yourself time to swing back."

This is where his depth in 70s, 80s and 90s catalogues becomes practical rather than nostalgic. "Those decades are the common language in a mixed-generation room. A well-placed classic can save a set faster than any transition trick."

## What Do You Actually Look For in the First Fifteen Minutes?

He breaks it down into a short list of signals he's trained himself to watch for over the years:

- **Body orientation** — are people facing the booth or facing each other, which tells you whether they're watching or just socializing.
- **Drink and service pace** — a bar that's slow usually means the room hasn't settled yet.
- **Who's dancing first** — the first three or four people on the floor usually predict who follows.
- **Phones out** — filming means the moment landed; scrolling means it hasn't.
- **Request patterns** — one or two requests is useful data; a flood means the set has drifted from the room.
- **Volume of conversation** — if the room gets louder than the music, energy is dropping, not rising.

"None of these alone tells you anything," he says. "Together, in the first fifteen minutes, they tell you almost everything about how the rest of the night needs to go."

## Does This Skill Transfer to Corporate Events?

"More than people expect," Tony says. "Corporate crowds in Doha or Beirut are reading you back — they're deciding whether this is background music or something they can actually engage with. You watch for when guests start moving closer to the dance area versus staying at their tables. That tells you when to push, and when a room simply wants ambience, which is its own kind of success."

## How Do You Prepare for a Room You've Never Played Before?

This is where preparation meets instinct, he says. "I always ask the planner or the couple beforehand — age range, cultural mix, whether it's more Arabic-leaning or more Western, indoor or outdoor. Then I build a library going in that covers every direction the night could take, so I'm never caught without the right track once I'm actually reading the room live."
