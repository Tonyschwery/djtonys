A few years ago, if you asked me to play Afrobeats at a Lebanese wedding, you'd get one or two requests, usually from a younger cousin who'd just come back from London. Now it's a standard part of the set — in Beirut, in Faqra, and increasingly in Doha and Dubai too. Artists like Burna Boy, Wizkid, Davido, Tems, and Rema aren't a niche request anymore. They're on the main playlist alongside Fairouz, French house, and dabke.

## What Is Afrobeats, Really?

Afrobeats — with an "s" — is the modern West African pop sound that grew out of Lagos and Accra, built on highlife, dancehall, and hip-hop rhythm, with a swung, syncopated groove that sits differently than straight-ahead house or Arabic pop. It's distinct from "Afrobeat," the older, horn-driven genre pioneered by Fela Kuti in the 70s. The two get confused constantly, but a DJ needs to know the difference, because they sit in completely different places in a set.

What made Afrobeats cross over globally is the same thing that makes it work at a mixed wedding in Beirut: it's danceable without being aggressive, it has space for vocals and ad-libs that crowds can sing along to even without knowing every lyric, and it pairs naturally with the call-and-response energy that Arabic weddings already run on.

## Why Afrobeats Works at Weddings in Beirut and Faqra

Lebanese wedding crowds are genuinely international — guests flying in from Europe, the Gulf, West Africa, North America. **A good Afrobeats moment gives every generation and every background a point of entry.** It's not as culturally loaded as an unfamiliar Arabic track for a foreign guest, and it's not as dated as a generic Top 40 pop song for a guest who wants something current.

I've found it works best in two specific windows:

1. **Mid-reception, after the formalities** — once the zaffe and first dance are done and the floor is warming up, a track like Rema's "Calm Down" or a Wizkid record bridges the gap between dinner energy and full dance-floor energy.
2. **Inside the main party set**, mixed between house and Arabic tracks, usually around 100-115 BPM, which happens to overlap nicely with slower house and mid-tempo Arabic pop — meaning the transitions don't need a hard tempo jump.

## Blending Afrobeats With Arabic and Western Sets

The technical challenge isn't the BPM — it's the groove. Afrobeats sits on a swung, log-drum-adjacent pocket that doesn't always lock with the straight four-on-the-floor feel of house, or the specific rhythmic patterns of dabke. A clean transition usually needs one of a few tools:

- **A short a cappella or vocal chop** layered over a familiar Arabic or house instrumental to bridge the two worlds without a jarring cut.
- **Tempo-matching through a percussion-only breakdown**, letting the groove shift gradually rather than all at once.
- **Key matching** using the Camelot wheel, the same method I use moving between house tracks, applied here to find Afrobeats cuts that sit harmonically close to whatever came before.

This is really the same skill set that powers the Amapiano and Afro house moments I've written about before — the log-drum family of South African and West African sounds has been steadily working its way into Beirut and Gulf sets for a couple of years now, and Afrobeats is really the pop-facing cousin of that same movement.

## Reading the Room Before You Drop It

Not every crowd wants it, and that's fine. A Faqra mountain wedding full of extended family might respond better to a classic dabke set with one or two Afrobeats tracks sprinkled in as a palate cleanser. A Mar Mikhael club night or a private party in Dubai Marina-adjacent venues, by contrast, can often sustain a full 20-minute Afrobeats run without losing anyone.

The read comes from watching who's already on the floor, not who's sitting at the tables. If the floor is full of guests under 35 and the energy is already building, that's your window. If the floor is still mostly older relatives doing dabke, hold it for later.

## Sourcing Clean, DJ-Ready Afrobeats Edits

One real practical problem with this genre: a lot of the biggest Afrobeats records run five, six minutes with long intros and outros that aren't built for mixing. For club and wedding sets, you need radio edits, clean intros, and extended versions that actually give you eight bars to work with before the vocal drops. Building that part of a library takes real time — I keep a dedicated folder just for log-drum and Afrobeats edits, organized separately from my house and Arabic crates, because the mixing approach is different for each.

## Booking a DJ Who Can Blend Afrobeats in Lebanon or the Gulf

If you're planning a wedding in Beirut or Faqra, a private party in Mar Mikhael, or a corporate night in Doha or Dubai, it's worth asking your DJ directly whether they program Afrobeats and how they'd blend it with the rest of the night. A DJ who's only comfortable in one lane — pure house, or pure Arabic pop — will either skip the moment entirely or play it as an awkward, isolated block that kills momentum rather than building it.

A few questions worth putting to any DJ you're considering:

- Can you show me an example of how you'd transition from Arabic pop into Afrobeats and back?
- Do you carry clean, extended versions of current Afrobeats records, or only radio edits?
- How do you decide when a crowd is ready for that sound versus when to hold it?
