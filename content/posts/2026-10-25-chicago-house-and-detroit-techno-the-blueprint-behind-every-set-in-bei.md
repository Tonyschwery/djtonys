Ask most people on a dance floor in Mar Mikhael where house music comes from and they'll guess Ibiza, or maybe Berlin. The real answer is Chicago. Ask where techno comes from and the guess is usually Germany. The real answer is Detroit. Every warm-up set I play, every peak-hour build I program, traces back to a handful of clubs and bedroom studios in those two cities in the late 1970s and early 1980s.

I think it's worth telling this story properly, because it explains *why* certain things work on a dance floor in Beirut or Doha and others don't. This isn't trivia. It's the blueprint.

## Where did house music actually come from?

House music grew out of Chicago's **Warehouse** club in the late 1970s, where DJ Frankie Knuckles was blending disco records, European synth tracks, and drum machine edits into something new. Disco had just been declared dead by a hostile American mainstream — famously, a 1979 stadium event in Chicago literally blew up a crate of disco records. But the music didn't die. It went underground, into Black and gay clubs, and it mutated.

What came out the other side was house: a stripped-down, repetitive, bass-heavy sound built around the drum machine, especially the Roland TR-808 and TR-909. DJs and producers like Knuckles, Larry Levan in New York, and later Marshall Jefferson and Steve "Silk" Hurley gave the genre its name and its first real hits. The track that's usually credited as the one that broke house internationally, Jefferson's "Move Your Body," came out in 1986.

## Where did techno come from?

Around the same time, in Detroit, three friends from Belleville High School — Juan Atkins, Derrick May, and Kevin Saunderson, later nicknamed the **Belleville Three** — were building something adjacent but distinct. They were obsessed with European electronic music, particularly Kraftwerk, and with the sound of a post-industrial city. Techno was colder, more mechanical, more futurist than house. Atkins' group Cybotron and later his solo work as Model 500 laid the groundwork; May's "Strings of Life" from 1987 became one of the genre's defining anthems.

Both cities were producing dance music by and for communities that mainstream American radio had shut out. That matters. House and techno weren't built for festival main stages. They were built for small rooms, long nights, and dancers who wanted to lose themselves for hours. That DNA is still in the music.

## Why does this history matter for a DJ in Beirut or Doha?

Because the fundamentals haven't changed. A good house or techno set still runs on:

- **Repetition with purpose** — small variations over a steady groove, not constant drops
- **The groove over the hook** — the bassline and kick drum carry the room, not the vocal
- **Patience** — an energy curve built over an hour does more than three sudden peaks
- **The drum machine's legacy** — most of what you hear in a modern Beirut club set, from tech house to Afro house, still leans on 808 and 909 sounds

When I'm building a set for a club night in Mar Mikhael or a late set at a private party in Doha, I'm still using that Chicago and Detroit framework, even when the track was released last month. The genres on top change — tech house, melodic techno, Afro house — but the structure underneath is the same one those two cities invented forty-plus years ago.

## How did this sound reach Lebanon and the Gulf?

House and techno spread to Europe first, through Ibiza, Manchester, and Berlin, in the late 1980s and early 1990s. From there it moved into the Mediterranean and Gulf club circuits over the following two decades, carried by touring DJs, record imports, and eventually the internet. Lebanon's own electronic scene — the parties that shaped venues across Beirut and later the mountain scene around Faqra — grew up on that European house and techno wave as much as on disco.

Today, the same lineage runs through Doha's lounges, Dubai's clubs, and Beirut's bar districts. A resident DJ in any of those cities is, whether they know it or not, still working inside a structure two cities in the American Midwest invented.

## What this means if you're booking a DJ

If you're planning a club night, a private party, or even a wedding after-party, knowing a little of this history helps you ask better questions:

1. **Ask what era and style the DJ actually specializes in.** "I play everything" sounds good, but a DJ who can tell you the difference between a Chicago house groove and a Detroit techno one usually has deeper crate knowledge across the board.
2. **Ask how they build energy over a set, not just which songs they play.** The house and techno tradition is about the arc of a night, not a playlist of hits.
3. **Ask about transitions.** Harmonic mixing and groove-matching came directly out of this lineage — it's the difference between a set that flows and one that lurches from track to track.
4. **If it's a themed night, ask for references.** A DJ who respects the originators — Knuckles, May, Jefferson, Saunderson, Atkins — will usually build a more honest, better-sequenced set than one chasing whatever's trending that week.

Building a proper house or techno set also means keeping a library that's organized by groove and key, not just genre tags.
