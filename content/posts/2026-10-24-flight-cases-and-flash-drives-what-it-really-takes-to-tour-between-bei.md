## What does it actually take for a DJ to work across Lebanon and the Gulf?

People ask me this more than almost anything else. They know I play in Beirut, Faqra, and Mar Mikhael one month and Doha or Dubai the next, and they assume it's as simple as booking a flight. It isn't. Every booking that crosses a border comes with its own small logistics puzzle, and solving it quietly, before anyone notices, is part of the job.

I've been doing this for over 25 years, and the travel side of the business has changed almost as much as the music has. Here's what actually goes into getting a Lebanese DJ and his set-up from one country to another without a single beat being lost.

## Gear: what travels and what doesn't

The biggest misconception is that a DJ just shows up with a laptop. In reality, most serious bookings — weddings, club nights, corporate events — specify equipment in the rider, and a touring DJ needs to know what's waiting at the venue versus what has to come in a case.

**What I usually carry:**

- A backup laptop with the full library mirrored, never just one machine
- Multiple USB drives, each prepped and tested independently
- Personal headphones — never assume the venue has a spare pair that fits your ear
- A small toolkit for cables, adapters, and connectors that always go missing at 11 p.m.
- Any personal controller pieces I'm attached to, packed in a hard case rather than a gig bag

**What I expect the venue or hire company to provide**, confirmed well in advance: Pioneer CDJs, a mixer, monitors, and a PA sized correctly for the room. This is exactly why the rider conversation matters before the contract is signed — it's cheaper and far less stressful to settle this on a call than to discover a gap at load-in.

## Prepping the music library for the road

This is where a lot of DJs underestimate the work. A wedding in Faqra and a club set in Mar Mikhael draw from overlapping but different libraries — Arabic classics, dabke, 70s and 80s staples, current house and Afro house, plus whatever the couple or the club specifically asked for. A corporate gala in Doha needs its own clean, family-friendly folder. None of this gets built on the plane.

My approach before any trip:

1. Pull the relevant crates for that specific event from the master library
2. Re-check every file's tags, key, and BPM — Rekordbox analysis done at home, not at the venue
3. Export to at least two USBs, tested on a second machine before I leave
4. Keep a cloud backup so a lost bag never means a lost set

If you're building or organizing your own library and want tracks that are already clean, properly tagged, and DJ-ready instead of pulled from random corners of the internet, a resource like [topdjcrates.com](https://topdjcrates.com) is worth knowing about — curated WAV packs save hours of prep time that most people don't realize a DJ spends before they ever touch a mixer.

## Visas, work permits, and timing

This is the part clients rarely see. Lebanon and the GCC countries each have their own entry requirements for performers, and they're not identical. Some bookings move through an agency or event company that handles paperwork as part of the contract; others need the DJ or the client to sort it directly. Either way, this is not something to leave until the week of the event.

**A few things I always tell event planners booking across borders:**

- Confirm early whether the booking needs a formal work permit or falls under a standard visit visa — this depends on the country and the nature of the event
- Corporate and club bookings in the Gulf often go through a local entertainment company that already has the right paperwork process in place
- Private events — weddings, private parties — usually move faster, but still need lead time once flights, customs for equipment, and hotel logistics are factored in
- Ramadan, major holidays, and peak wedding season (autumn and spring in Lebanon, cooler months in the Gulf) compress calendars fast, so timing requests get tighter the closer you get to the date

None of this needs to be the client's problem to solve. It's mine. But it's also why "can you confirm next week?" sometimes gets a longer answer than people expect — a confirmed date isn't just a date in a calendar, it's a date that works for flights, gear shipping, and paperwork all lining up.

## Why this matters for the person booking

If you're planning a wedding in Beirut, a private party in Faqra, or a corporate event in Doha and you're choosing between a local DJ and someone who travels, this is the real difference: experience across markets. Playing weddings in Lebanon and club nights in the Gulf back to back means constantly recalibrating — different crowd expectations, different acoustics, different cultural context for the same song. A DJ who does this regularly has already solved problems you'll never see, because the gear showed up, the files were ready, and the paperwork was done weeks before anyone walked in.

**What to ask a traveling DJ before you book:**

- What equipment do you bring versus expect on-site?
- How far in advance do you need the date confirmed if there's travel involved?
- Do you handle your own visa and work permit logistics, or does that run through an agency?
- Can you provide a backup plan if gear or travel is delayed?

Good answers to these questions tell you more about reliability than any demo mix will.
