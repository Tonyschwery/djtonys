Walk into almost any good bar in Mar Mikhael on a Thursday night and, somewhere between the Arabic pop and the house, you'll hear it: a filtered disco loop, a vocoder vocal, a bassline that sounds like it's being squeezed through a phone line. That's French Touch. It's nearly three decades old and it has never left a Lebanese dance floor for long.

As someone who has been building sets for **25 years**, across genres and generations, I get asked a lot why certain sounds age better than others. French house is one of the best examples I know.

## What Was the French Touch Sound, Exactly?

French Touch was the wave of house music that came out of Paris in the mid-to-late 90s — Daft Punk's *Homework*, Stardust's "Music Sounds Better With You," Cassius, Bob Sinclar, Modjo. The sound leaned hard on disco samples, heavy filtering, and a kind of romantic, almost cinematic warmth that separated it from the rawer Chicago and Detroit house that inspired it.

What made it travel so well outside France is the same thing that makes it work in Lebanon today:

- **It's built on disco**, which already has deep roots in Beirut nightlife going back decades.
- **It's melodic first, rhythmic second** — easier for a mixed-generation crowd to connect with than minimal or tech house.
- **It sits comfortably between 118 and 126 BPM**, a sweet spot that works for both a club set and a wedding reception.
- **It samples records your guests' parents might have owned**, which creates an instant, almost subconscious recognition.

## Why French House Still Works on a Beirut Dance Floor

Lebanon has one of the most musically literate crowds I've played for anywhere, including the Gulf. Audiences here grew up on a mix of Arabic pop, French chanson, Italian disco, and American radio — often all in the same household. That cross-pollination is exactly what French Touch was built from. It isn't a foreign import on a Beirut floor; it's a sound that already matches the city's own musical DNA.

It also solves a real programming problem. A lot of modern house and techno is designed for headphones and festival mains — it rewards patience and doesn't always translate to a room full of people who came out for a wedding, a corporate night, or a Friday in **Mar Mikhael**. French house does the opposite. It's instantly danceable, it's warm rather than cold, and it rarely alienates anyone standing at the bar deciding whether to come to the floor.

That's why I'll still drop a Stardust-style filtered loop into a private party set in **Faqra** or a late-night segment of a wedding in **Beirut**, and watch it land with people who weren't even alive when the record came out.

## Building a Set That Honors the Era Without Feeling Like a Tribute Night

The mistake a lot of DJs make with nostalgic genres is treating them as a block — twenty minutes of "old stuff," then back to whatever's current. That's not how French Touch earns its place. It works best woven in.

A few things I actually do when programming it:

1. **Use it as a bridge, not a destination.** French house sits naturally between disco-edit intros and modern house or Afro house later in the night. The filtered disco sound is the connective tissue.
2. **Match keys where it counts.** A lot of French house tracks are built on simple, major-key loops, which makes them forgiving for harmonic mixing — useful if you're already thinking in terms of the Camelot wheel for the rest of your set.
3. **Respect the groove, don't rush the BPM.** These records were made for a slow build. Pushing the tempo too early kills the feeling that made them work in the first place.
4. **Pick the edit, not just the original.** Club edits and extended mixes give you more room to blend than the radio versions most people streamed.

## What This Means for Your Event in Beirut, Faqra, or Mar Mikhael

If you're planning a wedding, a private party, or a club night and you want something that feels sophisticated rather than purely nostalgic, French house is worth asking your DJ about directly. It photographs well, it reads well on a dance floor full of mixed ages, and it gives a set texture that pure Arabic pop or pure modern house can't deliver alone.

A few practical questions worth raising when you're booking a DJ in Lebanon for this kind of sound:

- Does the DJ actually own clean, mixable versions of these tracks, or are they relying on YouTube rips?
- Can they blend French house into Arabic or Khaleeji segments without it feeling like a jarring genre switch?
- Have they played rooms with a similar crowd age range — a mix of 25 to 55, which is typical at most Lebanese weddings?

These are the kind of details that separate a playlist from a real set.
