The snow is back on Mzaar Kfardebian, and so is the question I get every November: can you do a chalet party up there? The answer is yes, and it's one of my favorite bookings of the year. But a mountain chalet is not a ballroom in Beirut or a beach club in Batroun. The altitude, the cold, and the layout of a typical chalet change how I set up and how I program the night.

## Mzaar Kfardebian isn't just Faqra's neighbor

People often lump the whole Mount Lebanon ski belt together, but Mzaar Kfardebian — sitting above Faraya, roughly 40 minutes from Beirut — is its own world. It's home to Lebanon's largest ski resort, and the village below it fills up every winter weekend with families and groups renting chalets for the season. Faqra has its own private, gated character and its own events calendar. Mzaar Kfardebian is louder, busier, more ski-town. That difference matters when you're booking a DJ, because the two settings call for different energy from the first song.

A Faqra private party often starts quiet and builds slowly in a controlled indoor space. A Mzaar chalet party usually starts with people walking in straight off the slopes, boots still half-on, looking for music that matches the adrenaline they already have. I program for that. The first thirty minutes can carry more energy here than almost anywhere else I play.

## What actually changes at altitude

A few things I've learned over years of winter bookings in this belt:

- **Power is less predictable than in the city.** Mountain chalets run on a mix of grid power and generators, and voltage can dip when everyone's heating, lighting, and sound system are pulling at once. I always ask about the electrical setup before load-in and bring my own power conditioning, not just my gear.
- **Cold affects equipment.** Pioneer CDJs, laptops, and controllers don't love sudden temperature swings. Gear that travels from a warm car trunk into a freezing chalet terrace needs time to acclimate before I push it hard. I build that into my arrival time.
- **Sound travels differently in snow.** Snow actually absorbs high frequencies, which softens a room in a way wood and stone don't. I adjust my EQ on site rather than trusting a setting that worked at a wedding in Beirut the week before.
- **Space is tighter.** Most chalets weren't built with a DJ booth in mind. I scout the layout — staircases, low ceilings, where the speakers can actually throw sound without blowing out the people standing nearest them — before I even think about music.
- **Neighbors are close.** Chalet villages are dense, and sound carries across the snow at night more than people expect. A curfew conversation with the host before the party saves everyone a headache.

## Building the playlist for a snow day

Ski season parties in Mzaar Kfardebian tend to fall into two shapes, and I program them differently.

**Afternoon après-ski sets** are about momentum. People are coming off the mountain, still buzzing, often in groups that don't all know each other yet. I keep the energy up with recognizable, feel-good tracks — a mix of current dance hits, 90s and 2000s staples, and the kind of Arabic and Western crossover that gets everyone moving without needing an introduction. This is not the place for a slow build. It's instant warmth after a cold day.

**Evening chalet parties** give me more room to shape a real arc. These run longer, the crowd is usually one group that knows each other well, and requests come fast. I'll open warmer, lean into nostalgia — 80s and 90s records are almost always the biggest reaction of the night in these settings — and build toward a peak around midnight before easing into a lounge-paced closing hour. Fireplace, mulled wine, and a slow house groove is a combination that works every single season.

If you're a host putting together your own pre-party playlist before the DJ arrives, or just want a sense of how a winter set is structured, building a short crate of key winter-mood tracks ahead of time is worth doing properly rather than relying on a generic streaming mix. ## Corporate ski days are growing too

It's not just private chalets. Companies based in Beirut increasingly book a day on the mountain as a team event — lunch, skiing or snow activities, then music in the late afternoon as people come down. These need a different conversation than a party: shorter set times, background-level energy during lunch, a clear ramp-up window, and often a strict end time because buses back to Beirut are waiting. I treat these more like a corporate event than a club booking, because that's what they are.

## What to ask before you book a DJ for a mountain event

If you're planning a chalet party or ski-day event in Mzaar Kfardebian or Faraya this season, a few questions will save you trouble:

1. **What's the power situation** — grid, generator, or both, and has it handled a full sound system before?
2. **How is the space laid out**, and is there a realistic spot for a booth that doesn't block foot traffic?
3. **What time does the party actually need to peak** — afternoon arrival energy or a late-night build?
4. **Is there a curfew** from the chalet complex or neighboring properties?
5. **How will gear get up the mountain** — road conditions matter more here than almost any other booking in Lebanon.

A DJ who's played Beirut weddings, Faqra private parties, and Gulf club residencies should be able to answer all of this without blinking. If they can't, that's worth knowing before the first snowfall, not during the party.

If you're planning something on the mountain this winter, reach out early. Road access and daylight hours are tighter up there, and the good weekends book fast.
