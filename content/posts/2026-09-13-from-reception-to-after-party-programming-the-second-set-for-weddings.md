Every Lebanese wedding has a shape. Zaffe, first dance, dinner, cake, the parents' dance, the open floor. By the time the formal program winds down, most couples assume the night is basically over. It isn't — not if the DJ plans for what comes next.

More couples in **Beirut and Faqra** are now building a real after-party into the evening: a second set, often in a different room or a different venue entirely, that runs later and hits harder than anything before it. It's one of the most requested additions I get asked about when couples sit down to plan their music, and it deserves its own conversation, separate from the main reception timeline.

## What Actually Counts as an After-Party Set

The reception set is built for everyone — grandparents, cousins, coworkers, the whole guest list. It has to work for a wide range of ages and tastes, which means the energy curve rises and falls a lot: a slow song here, a dabke set there, a request from the table of aunts.

The after-party is different. By the time it starts, the guest list has usually thinned out to the people who actually want to keep dancing. That means:

- **Fewer genre detours.** You can commit to a sound — house, Arabic remix-driven open format, disco-funk, whatever the couple loves — and stay there.
- **A steadier BPM climb.** No need to drop back down for a slow song every twenty minutes.
- **Longer blends.** Tracks can breathe. A DJ can extend a groove for six or seven minutes instead of cutting after three.

I treat it almost like a short club set dropped into the middle of a wedding night. Same instincts I'd use in a **Mar Mikhael** bar or a Beirut club, just compressed into an hour or two.

## Why Faqra and Beirut Are Different Rooms for This

The mountain venues in **Faqra** usually mean the after-party stays in the same space — a chalet terrace, a tent, a lounge area that empties out as the older guests head home. The acoustics are more forgiving outdoors, but the cold mountain air late at night changes the mood too. Sets there tend to lean warmer: disco, funk-driven house, familiar 90s records that keep people moving without needing a packed floor to feel full.

In **Beirut**, it's common for the after-party to physically move — the reception ends at one venue and a smaller group heads to a club or a late bar. That's a completely different programming problem. You're not gradually raising energy from a wedding baseline anymore; you're walking into a room that's already primed for a club environment, so the set can open harder and stay there.

Either way, the couple's job is to tell the DJ which version they want. That's a conversation worth having weeks before the wedding, not on the night itself.

## What to Ask Your DJ Before the Big Day

If you're planning a wedding in Beirut, Faqra, or anywhere in Lebanon and you want a real after-party, not just "the DJ keeps playing," bring these questions to your first meeting:

1. **Is the after-party staying in the same room, or moving?** This changes the sound system needs and the transition plan entirely.
2. **What's the actual end time?** Curfews and venue contracts vary, and the set has to be built to land, not just stop.
3. **Do you want a genre shift, or more of the same energy?** Some couples want the after-party to feel like a continuation; others want it to feel like a completely different night.
4. **Should older guests be gently signaled that the vibe is changing?** A good DJ can do this with music alone — no announcement needed.
5. **Is there a second crate prepared, or is it the same set extended?** These require different prep.

That last point matters more than people think. A DJ who's prepared two distinct pools of music — one for the reception, one for the after-party — gives you a night that actually builds rather than one that just runs long.

## The Prep Behind the Second Set

This is where the craft lives. I keep a separate folder in Rekordbox specifically for after-party material: tracks with longer intros and outros built for extended mixing, records that don't need lyrics everyone recognizes, cuts that work purely on groove. It's a different kind of curation than a wedding reception library, which has to be built for recognition and singalong moments.

If you're a DJ building out that kind of library yourself, sourcing clean, well-tagged, DJ-ready tracks matters more than people realize — a set only flows as well as the files behind it. ## Making the Transition Feel Natural

The worst version of an after-party is a hard stop and restart — house lights up, DJ swaps gear, guests stand around awkwardly for ten minutes. The best version flows. That usually means:

- Starting the shift a few songs before the "official" reception ends, so the energy is already climbing when the formal part closes.
- Using a track everyone knows as the bridge — something familiar enough to keep the room together before the sound gets more specific.
- Having the gear and next set of tracks already loaded and ready, whether that's a second CDJ session cued up or a controller pre-loaded with the after-party folder.

Done right, guests don't notice the switch happened. They just realize, an hour later, that the night got better.
