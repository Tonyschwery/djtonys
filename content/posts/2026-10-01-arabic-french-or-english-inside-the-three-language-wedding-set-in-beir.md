No other country mixes three languages into one wedding night quite like Lebanon. Arabic for the family, French for the grandparents and the older crowd, English for the cousins who grew up abroad. One dance floor, three vocabularies, and a DJ who has to move between all of them without anyone feeling left out.

I sat down with Tony Schwery to talk about how he actually handles this in practice — not as a theory, but as something that happens every weekend in Beirut and up in Faqra.

## Why is Lebanon's wedding crowd trilingual in the first place?

"It's just how Lebanon grew up," Tony says. "French came from the Mandate era and it's still the language a lot of families use at home, especially the older generation. English came later, through schools, through television, through everyone who studied or worked abroad. And Arabic is always there underneath — it's the heart language. A wedding in Beirut or Faqra isn't one crowd. It's three generations and three language habits sitting at the same tables."

This is why **a wedding DJ in Lebanon needs more than a good playlist** — he needs to understand who responds to what, and when.

## Does the language of a song matter more than the song itself?

"Sometimes, yes. A French chanson can completely change the energy for the older tables even if the tempo doesn't match anything you'd play ten minutes later. An English pop track that the younger cousins know word for word will get hands up immediately, but it might do nothing for the table of uncles. Arabic — dabke, tarab, the newer pop — that's usually the common ground. Almost everyone in the room has some connection to it."

Tony's approach:

- **Start Arabic-forward** during the early reception — it signals "this is a Lebanese wedding" and brings older guests onto the floor early.
- **Layer in French during slower transitions** — dinner service, speeches, the moments between sets. It keeps the older generation comfortable without stopping the night.
- **Save English for the peak energy windows** — later in the night when the crowd has loosened up and the younger guests are driving the floor.
- **Never commit to one language for more than fifteen or twenty minutes** — the room shifts, and the set has to shift with it.

"It's not about dividing the night into three blocks," he adds. "It's about weaving. You might go Arabic, Arabic, French, Arabic, English, Arabic. You're reading faces, not following a script."

## What does the MC side of this actually sound like?

A lot of people assume this is purely a music question, but Tony points out it's just as much about how announcements are handled. "Introducing the couple, calling people up for the zaffe, announcing the cake — all of that happens in whatever mix of languages the family actually speaks. Some families want everything bilingual, Arabic and English. Some want French in there too, especially for anything formal. I always ask the couple ahead of time: who's going to be in the room, and what language makes your grandmother feel included? That's usually the real answer."

This is one of the first things Tony asks on an initial call with a couple planning a wedding in Beirut or a mountain celebration in Faqra — alongside guest count, venue layout, and whether there's a live band sharing the night with the DJ set.

## How do you avoid losing the dance floor when you switch languages?

"BPM and key are still the backbone," Tony says. "Language is layered on top of the mix, not instead of it. If I'm moving from an Arabic pop track at 128 BPM into an English house edit, the tempo and energy have to line up or the floor feels the jump even if they don't consciously notice the language change. The transition has to be invisible. People should just feel like the night keeps getting better — they shouldn't be able to point to the exact second it changed."

This is part of why **building a properly tagged music library** matters so much for this kind of set. ## Does this change between a Beirut hotel ballroom and a Faqra outdoor wedding?

"A little. In Faqra, you're often outdoors, acoustics are more open, and the crowd skews toward a younger, more international mix — a lot of Gulf and diaspora guests who grew up more English-dominant. In a traditional Beirut ballroom wedding, especially one rooted in Mar Mikhael or downtown family circles, you'll often lean more Arabic and French overall, with English reserved for the late-night stretch. Neither is a rule. It's a starting read, and then you adjust in real time."

## What should a couple tell their DJ before the wedding?

Tony's advice for couples booking a DJ anywhere in Lebanon or the Gulf:

1. **Tell him who's actually coming** — ages, backgrounds, how many guests are local versus diaspora.
2. **Flag any language sensitivities** — some families want announcements strictly bilingual, others want everything in Arabic with English only for younger guests.
3. **Share a short list of must-plays and must-avoids** — in any language, this saves awkward moments on the night.
4. **Trust the read in the room** — the final sequencing should flex to the actual crowd, not just the plan on paper.

"At the end of the day," Tony says, "the language is just the surface. What people really respond to is feeling seen. A grandmother hearing a French song she danced to at her own wedding. A cousin from London hearing the English track that was playing the summer she fell in love with someone. That's the job — finding the right language, right song, right moment, for every single person on that floor."
