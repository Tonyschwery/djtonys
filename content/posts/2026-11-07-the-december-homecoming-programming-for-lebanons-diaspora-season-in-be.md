Ask anyone who runs a bar in Mar Mikhael and they'll tell you: the room changes in December. The regulars are still there, but they're outnumbered by faces you haven't seen since last Christmas — cousins flying in from Montreal, a childhood friend now living in Lagos, an aunt who left Beirut in the nineties and hasn't missed a homecoming since. For a few weeks, Lebanon fills back up with the people who carry it with them wherever they've gone. And every single one of them wants to hear something different on the dance floor.

I've built a career around reading rooms, and the diaspora season is the hardest room there is. It's also one of the best.

## Why December Is Its Own Kind of Night

A wedding in Faqra in September is one crowd — local, familiar, predictable in the good sense. A Thursday night in Mar Mikhael in July is another — younger, more club-literate, tuned into whatever's moving on TikTok that week. December collapses all of that into a single dance floor. You get:

- **Lebanese expats** who left at different points in the last three decades, each carrying a different "home" soundtrack with them
- **Second-generation kids** who grew up abroad hearing Fairouz and Sabah on their parents' phones, not live
- **Local friends and family** who never left and expect the set to reflect *now*, not a nostalgia act
- **International partners and friends** tagging along, hearing Lebanese music in a Lebanese room for the first time

That's not one crowd. It's four crowds sharing a dance floor for one night, and your set has to serve all of them without feeling like four separate playlists stapled together.

## Programming for a Room That Spans Three Continents

The instinct is to split the night into blocks — Arabic set, then international, then back again. That works in theory, but it tends to create a stop-start energy. What actually holds a diaspora room together is **finding the tracks that already live in everyone's memory at once**: the Fairouz and Sabah classics that every generation grew up on, the French house and disco edits that defined Beirut's clubs in the 2000s, the dabke tracks that never needed translation to begin with.

I build these nights in layers rather than blocks:

1. **An early layer of warmth** — golden-age Arabic classics and familiar 80s and 90s tracks that get people talking and ordering their first round, not yet dancing hard
2. **A rising layer of shared references** — French touch, disco edits, the kind of house music that was playing in Beirut clubs when half the room still lived here
3. **A peak layer built on request-table intelligence** — reading what the Montreal cousin is asking for versus what the Lagos friend is asking for, and finding the track that satisfies both
4. **A closing layer that belongs only to Lebanon** — dabke, tarab, the songs that make people who haven't seen each other in a year grab hands and form a circle

That closing layer is non-negotiable. Whatever happens in the middle of the night, a diaspora crowd wants to end feeling unmistakably home.

## The Request List Gets More Interesting, Not Harder

A normal Thursday request table is predictable — a run of current Arabic pop, maybe a viral TikTok track, a classic thrown in by someone's dad. In December, the requests come from everywhere. Someone wants a track that's huge in the Gulf right now. Someone else wants a song that was big in Beirut the year they left and hasn't aged a day in their memory, even if the local crowd has moved on from it. Someone's partner, visiting Lebanon for the first time, just wants to feel the room.

Handling that well means **having a library deep enough to answer a request from any decade and any diaspora city**, not just whatever's charting this month. This is where crate preparation actually earns its keep — clean edits of old favorites, extended versions of the tracks that need room to breathe, and enough variety across eras that nothing in the set feels like it's catering to only one generation. ## What This Means If You're Booking for a December Event

If you're hosting a family gathering, a reunion, or a private party in Beirut, Faqra, or Mar Mikhael this season, a few things are worth asking your DJ before the night:

- **Can they build a set that moves across decades without losing energy?** This season punishes a one-era set more than any other time of year.
- **Do they have deep enough Arabic and Western crossover material** to serve guests who've been away as easily as guests who never left?
- **Can they read a room that's genuinely mixed** — age, geography, musical reference points — and adjust in real time rather than running a fixed playlist?

A diaspora crowd is forgiving of almost everything except a DJ who plays it safe and narrow. They've traveled a long way to be in that room. The set should feel like it was built for exactly that.
