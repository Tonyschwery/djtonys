Ask anyone who was in a club in the late 90s or early 2000s what they remember, and it's rarely the venue. It's a breakdown. A build. The moment a trance anthem drops back into the beat and the whole room lifts off the floor at once. That feeling didn't disappear — it's just been waiting for the right room to come back into.

I released music through Blackhole Recordings, one of the labels that defined that era, and I still feel the pull of that sound every time I play a late set in **Mar Mikhael** or close out a night at a private party in **Faqra**. Trance isn't nostalgia anymore. It's back in rotation, and it's doing real work on dance floors across Lebanon and the Gulf.

## What Was Blackhole Recordings, Really?

Blackhole Recordings grew out of the Dutch scene in the late 90s, part of a wave of labels that turned uplifting trance into a global export. It became home to some of the genre's most recognizable sub-labels and gave early releases to artists who went on to headline festivals worldwide. The sound was built on a specific formula: long intros for mixing, a melodic breakdown that let the crowd breathe, and a climax engineered to hit exactly when the lights do.

That formula is why trance records from that era still mix so cleanly into modern sets. They were built by DJs, for DJs. The structure respects the person behind the decks as much as the person on the floor.

## Why Trance Is Resurfacing in Lebanon Right Now

A few things are happening at once:

- **A generation that grew up on it is now the one booking DJs.** Couples planning weddings in Faqra and corporate clients in Beirut are often the same people who were on the floor for these records the first time around.
- **Modern production has made old trance new again.** Remixes, edits, and extended rework culture mean a 2001 anthem can sit comfortably next to a 2026 progressive house record without sounding dated.
- **Melodic techno opened the door.** A lot of what's filling rooms in Dubai and Doha right now shares DNA with trance — driving arpeggios, emotional builds, big room dynamics. Crowds raised on that sound are primed for the real thing.

## How Trance Fits Into a Lebanese Night Out

Trance was never background music, and that's exactly why it needs careful placement. I don't open with it in Mar Mikhael — a bar crowd needs to settle in first. It works best as a **peak-time tool**, deployed when the room has already committed to dancing and needs a reason to go further.

For a wedding in Faqra, it almost never appears during dinner or the first dance. It belongs in the after-party set, once the older guests have said their goodbyes and the dance floor has turned into something closer to a club. A well-placed trance breakdown at 1 a.m., timed with the lighting, can do more for a room than three hours of generic house.

### Reading when a crowd is ready for it

A few signs I look for before reaching for a trance record:

1. The floor is already full and moving, not just standing near the booth.
2. The requests have shifted from song titles to vibes — people asking to "keep it going" rather than naming a track.
3. There's a visible age range on the floor old enough to recognize the build and young enough to have discovered it through a remix or a festival set.

If those three line up, the breakdown lands. If they don't, it's better saved for later.

## Programming Trance Without Losing the Room

The mistake I see most often — and it's an easy one to make — is treating trance as a genre to play rather than a tool to use. A full trance set works in very specific rooms: a dedicated club night, a festival stage, a residency built around that sound. For most private events, weddings, and corporate parties across Beirut, Doha, and Dubai, it works better woven in: one or two carefully chosen tracks inside a broader open-format set, used to mark a turning point in the night rather than to define the whole evening.

Key and BPM matching matters even more here than with house or Arabic pop transitions. Trance tracks often run in the 132–140 BPM range with long harmonic builds, which means the records before and after need to be chosen with real care — not just mixed, but matched in key so the transition feels inevitable rather than jarring. ## What This Means for Booking a DJ in Lebanon or the Gulf

If you're planning a night in **Mar Mikhael**, a wedding after-party in **Faqra**, or a private event anywhere from Beirut to Doha, trance isn't something to request outright — it's something to trust your DJ to place correctly. The right person will know the difference between a crowd that's ready for a breakdown and one that just wants another Arabic classic.

What's worth asking when you're booking:

- Does the DJ have real catalog depth across eras, not just a current Top 40 playlist?
- Can they explain how they'd build toward a peak moment rather than just naming genres they "play"?
- Have they actually worked rooms in Lebanon's bar districts, where the energy shifts fast and the set has to shift with it?

Twenty-five years in, I still think the best compliment a dance floor can give a DJ is forgetting what time it is. Trance, placed right, does exactly that.
