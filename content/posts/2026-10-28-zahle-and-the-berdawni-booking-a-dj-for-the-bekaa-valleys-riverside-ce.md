Ask anyone from the Bekaa where to celebrate a family milestone, and they'll point you toward the Berdawni. Zahle's river-lined strip of open-air restaurants has been the backdrop for engagements, baptisms, anniversaries, and long Sunday lunches for generations. It's one of Lebanon's oldest continuous dining traditions — and one of the least understood when it comes to sound.

I get asked to play Zahle a few times a year, usually for family engagement parties or a corporate retreat that wants something more memorable than a Beirut ballroom. It's a different kind of booking than a Faqra wedding or a Mar Mikhael club night, and it rewards a DJ who understands the difference.

## What makes the Berdawni different

The Berdawni restaurants sit in a long row along the river, terrace after terrace, often with shared outdoor space and neighboring venues within earshot. That changes everything about how I plan a set.

- **The crowd spans three generations easily.** A Zahle engagement party might have grandparents at one table and cousins in their twenties at the next. The music has to move between both without losing either.
- **Acoustics are open-air and linear**, not boxed in like a ballroom. Sound travels down the river corridor, which means volume and speaker placement matter more than usual — you want coverage across a long terrace, not one loud zone near the booth.
- **Neighboring restaurants are playing music too.** Spacing and directional sound keep your event from clashing with the party three tables down.
- **Food is central, not a break in the night.** Mezze service in the Bekaa can run long and unhurried. The music underneath it needs patience — warm, textured, never pushing the room before it's ready.

## Programming for a multigenerational riverside crowd

This is where 25 years of playing everything from 70s soul to 90s dance actually pays off. A Zahle celebration isn't a club set building toward one peak at 2 a.m. It's a long arc that has to satisfy very different ears over several hours.

My approach usually looks like this:

1. **Arrival and mezze** — Fairouz, Wadih El Safi, and Lebanese golden-age classics set a warm, familiar tone while people settle in and greet each other.
2. **Mid-meal** — a blend of Arabic pop and soft Western classics, kept conversational, nothing that competes with people talking across a long table.
3. **Main course into dessert** — this is where I start layering in dabke rhythms and upbeat Arabic tracks, testing the room for who's ready to get up.
4. **The turn** — once the older generation has had their moment on the floor, I shift toward a livelier mix: dabke, Khaleeji-influenced tracks if the crowd calls for it, and crowd-pleasing 80s and 90s hits that everyone from 20 to 70 knows the words to.
5. **Late night** — if the venue and neighbors allow it, a tighter, more club-leaning stretch for whoever's still dancing.

A dabke set built for a riverside terrace is different from one built for an indoor wedding hall — the energy has to rise more gradually, since you're often working with families seated at long shared tables rather than a dance floor crowd standing and ready to move.

## Booking considerations specific to the Bekaa

If you're planning a celebration along the Berdawni or elsewhere in Zahle, a few things are worth sorting out early:

- **Confirm outdoor power access.** Many riverside terraces weren't built with a full DJ setup in mind — Pioneer CDJs, a mixer, and proper speakers need a stable, dedicated supply, not a shared outlet running the kitchen's fridges too.
- **Ask about neighboring events.** If the terrace next door is hosting a party the same night, a quick conversation about timing and volume saves everyone a headache.
- **Weather is a real factor.** The Bekaa gets cold fast once the sun drops, even in shoulder seasons — plan for covered or heated seating if your event runs into the evening.
- **Build in flexibility for the guest list.** Family events in Zahle often run larger and less predictable than a fixed wedding guest count. A good DJ reads the room and adjusts rather than sticking rigidly to a pre-built playlist.

## Why this matters beyond Zahle

The riverside restaurant model isn't unique to the Bekaa — it's a version of the same challenge I handle for rooftop events in Doha, waterfront parties in Beirut, or outdoor weddings in Faqra. Open-air sound, mixed generations, and food-forward timelines come up constantly across Lebanon and the Gulf. What changes is the cultural texture: more Fairouz and dabke in the Bekaa, more Khaleeji influence in Doha, more French-Arabic crossover in Beirut.

What stays the same is the job: build a library deep enough to move between eras and languages without a dead moment, and read the room closely enough to know when it's time to shift.
