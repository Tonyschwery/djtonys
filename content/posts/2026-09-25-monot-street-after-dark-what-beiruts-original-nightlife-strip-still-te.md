Ask anyone in Beirut over forty where the city went out at night in the mid-90s and early 2000s, and the answer is usually the same: Monot. Long before Gemmayze filled up, before Mar Mikhael became the city's go-to bar strip, Monot Street in Achrafieh was Beirut's nightlife address. It's a smaller, quieter part of the city now — but it shaped how a generation of Lebanese DJs, myself included, learned to read a room.

## What was Monot Street in Beirut?

Monot rose in the years after the civil war, when Beirut was rebuilding and rediscovering its nightlife almost neighborhood by neighborhood. The street sat close to Université Saint-Joseph and pulled in students, young professionals, and a crowd that wanted something between a bar and a club — small rooms, tight dance floors, and DJs who had to work a lot harder for a lot less space than you'd get downtown or on the coast.

That's the part people forget. Monot wasn't built for massive sound systems and headline sets. It was built for **close-range energy** — a room where the DJ could see every face on the floor and adjust in real time. If you wanted to test whether you actually knew how to read a crowd, not just play a playlist, Monot was where you found out.

## Why did Beirut's nightlife move to other neighborhoods?

Nightlife in Beirut has always moved. Downtown had its run, then Gemmayze picked up the heritage-bar crowd, Mar Mikhael became the industrial-chic strip everyone knows now, and Hamra held onto its own bohemian identity the whole time. Monot never fully disappeared — a handful of bars and lounges still hold the block — but the center of gravity shifted east and toward the water.

None of that makes Monot's era less relevant. If anything, the neighborhoods that came after it inherited its format: **small, dense venues where the DJ is close enough to touch the crowd**, not playing from a stage twenty meters back. That's still how most of Beirut's best nights work, and it's very different from programming a wedding ballroom in Faqra or a beach lounge in Batroun.

## What a small-room strip like Monot teaches about booking a DJ

The lesson from Monot's heyday applies directly to anyone booking a DJ for a bar, a private party, or an intimate venue anywhere in Beirut today:

1. **Room size changes everything.** A DJ who only knows how to fill a 2,000-capacity club floor can struggle in a 60-person bar. Reading a small room means smaller, faster adjustments — you notice a dip in energy after one bad transition, not after ten minutes.
2. **Open format matters more than genre depth.** In a tight venue, the crowd shifts from disco to Arabic pop to 2000s R&B in the same hour. A DJ needs the range to move between them without losing the floor.
3. **Sound has to match the space.** What sounds great through a club rig will blow out a small room. Getting levels right for a bar-sized venue is its own skill, separate from big-room mixing.
4. **The DJ becomes part of the room, not separate from it.** In an intimate venue, there's no barrier between booth and crowd. Requests, reactions, and mood shifts happen right in front of you, all night.

I've spent 25 years moving between exactly these kinds of spaces — from small Beirut bars to bigger festival stages across the Middle East and Europe. The skills don't transfer automatically. A great big-room set doesn't shrink down cleanly into a great small-room one, and the reverse is just as true.

## Booking a DJ for a bar, lounge, or intimate venue in Beirut

If you're planning a private party, a bar takeover, or a smaller event anywhere from Achrafieh to Mar Mikhael, here's what actually matters when you're choosing a DJ:

- **Ask what venue sizes they've worked.** A resident who's only played large clubs may need direction on reading a smaller floor.
- **Talk through the sound setup in advance.** Bars and lounges often have limited or shared systems — the DJ should know how to work with what's there, not fight it.
- **Confirm genre range, not just one sound.** Intimate Beirut crowds jump eras and languages fast. You want someone comfortable moving from 90s classics to current Arabic and Western hits without a hard stop.
- **Ask how they handle proximity to the crowd.** In a small room, the DJ takes requests, reads faces, and adjusts constantly. That's a different skill than commanding a stage from a distance.
