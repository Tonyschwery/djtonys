Every DJ has a sound that shaped how they hear music. For me, part of that education came through releasing on **Inkognito Records**, a label built on deep house — the kind of groove that sits between soulful and stripped-back, never rushing, always moving. Twenty-five years into this career, I still build sets around that same logic. Here's why it still works on a dance floor in Beirut or Mar Mikhael tonight.

## What makes deep house different

Deep house doesn't chase the drop. It's built on a steady 4/4 kick, warm basslines, jazz and soul chord voicings, and vocals that breathe rather than shout. Where big-room house or festival trance is designed to peak hard and fast, deep house is designed to hold a room in a groove and let it build slowly, sometimes over twenty or thirty minutes.

That patience is the real lesson. A lot of DJs starting out want to play their biggest record in the first hour. Deep house teaches you the opposite: **the room rewards restraint**. You let a bassline sit. You let a vocal loop breathe before you bring in the next layer. The crowd stays on the floor longer because nothing feels forced.

## Why this matters for a night in Mar Mikhael or Beirut

Mar Mikhael's bar scene is intimate. People are standing close to the booth, drinks in hand, and the room often shifts in waves rather than one long climax. A deep house foundation is ideal here because it lets me read the crowd in real time — nudging the energy up or down without ever breaking the groove the room has settled into.

Beirut's bigger clubs work differently; they want a full arc across a night. But even there, I'll often open or close with deep house, because it's the sound that gets a room moving without demanding attention. It's background enough to let conversation happen early in the night, and foreground enough to carry a packed floor at 2 a.m.

## The gear and the groove

Deep house rewards a DJ who actually blends, not just cues up the next track. A few things I rely on:

- **Long, overlapping transitions** — thirty seconds to a minute of two tracks playing together, not a quick cut.
- **EQ work over the crossfader** — pulling bass out of the incoming track before it clashes with the one still playing.
- **BPM range discipline** — most deep house sits in the 118–124 range, which is close enough across tracks that Rekordbox and Pioneer CDJs make tempo-matching almost automatic, leaving more attention for the actual mix.
- **Vocal phrasing** — bringing a vocal in on the right bar, not just the right beat, so it feels intentional rather than accidental.

None of that is unique to deep house, but it's where I learned to do it properly, because the genre doesn't forgive sloppy transitions the way a louder, busier style might.

## Building a deep house crate

If you're a DJ trying to build this sound into your own sets, the hard part isn't finding one good deep house track — it's finding twenty that sit together well in key and energy, so a set doesn't feel like a random playlist. I still spend real time organizing a library by key, BPM, and mood rather than just genre tags, because that's what makes a long, groove-based set actually flow. ## Does this translate to weddings and private events?

Yes, and it's often underused. A lot of wedding sets in Beirut and Faqra jump straight from dinner music to high-energy Arabic and Western hits. But a deep house stretch — maybe during cocktail hour, or as the floor is building after the first dance — gives guests a smoother on-ramp. It's sophisticated without being boring, and it sets up a much bigger payoff when the energy does climb later in the night.

For corporate events in Doha or private parties anywhere in the Gulf, the same logic applies. Deep house works as a sound that lets people network, eat, and talk comfortably, then gradually shifts the room toward the dance floor without anyone noticing the exact moment it happened.

## A few questions worth asking your DJ

If you're booking entertainment for a wedding, private party, or corporate event and want a set with this kind of range, here's what's worth asking:

1. **Can you build a set that moves between genres without jarring transitions?** Deep house is a good test — it requires real blending skill, not just track selection.
2. **Do you read the room live, or follow a fixed set list?** The best deep house sets are built in response to the crowd, not pre-programmed.
3. **What's your library organization like?** A DJ who has their tracks sorted by key and energy, not just folder names, can pivot faster when the room needs it.
4. **Can you handle the full range — lounge, deep house, Arabic classics, and peak-time hits — in one night?** That versatility is what a 25-year career across genres is actually for.
