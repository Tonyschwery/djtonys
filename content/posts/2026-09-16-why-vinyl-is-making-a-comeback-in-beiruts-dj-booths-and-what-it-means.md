Walk into a handful of bars in Mar Mikhael on any given weekend and you'll notice something that would have seemed out of place ten years ago: turntables, actual crates of records, and a DJ cueing up a track by ear instead of a waveform. Vinyl never fully disappeared from Beirut, but it's clearly having a moment again — and it's changing what people expect from a night out, and from the DJ they book.

## Why is vinyl coming back in Beirut's bars and clubs?

Part of it is global. Vinyl has been on a slow, steady climb everywhere for over a decade, driven by collectors, younger listeners discovering it for the first time, and DJs who missed the physicality of it. Beirut's scene tends to move with global nightlife culture faster than people expect, and Mar Mikhael — with its density of small, independent bars — is exactly the kind of neighborhood where that shows up first. It's easier to build a listening-bar atmosphere in a 40-capacity room on Armenia Street than it is in a 1,000-capacity club.

There's also a local instinct at play. Lebanon has always had a deep, specific relationship with **70s, 80s, and 90s music** — disco, Italo, early house, the golden era of Arabic pop crossed with Western sounds. A lot of that material lives most authentically on wax. Playing it off vinyl isn't nostalgia for its own sake. It's about texture — the warmth, the slight imperfection, the fact that the DJ is physically riding the record rather than letting software do the beatmatching.

## What vinyl actually changes about a DJ set

For a listener, the difference is subtle but real:

- **The pacing is different.** You can't scroll a laptop screen and drop the next track in two seconds. Vinyl sets tend to breathe more, with the DJ committing to a track and building the room around it rather than chasing instant reactions.
- **The selection is narrower and more deliberate.** A crate of 40-60 records forces real curation before the night even starts. There's no searching a 40,000-track library mid-set.
- **The sound has character.** Analog warmth is a real, audible thing, especially through a good soundsystem in an intimate room.
- **Mistakes are part of the charm.** A slightly rushed transition on vinyl reads as human. The same on a controller reads as sloppy. Audiences forgive vinyl differently.

None of this makes vinyl "better" than digital — it makes it a different tool for a different kind of night.

## Does this matter if you're booking a DJ for a wedding or private event?

Mostly, no — and that's worth saying plainly. A wedding in Faqra or a corporate event in downtown Beirut needs reliability above almost everything else: **Pioneer CDJs and Rekordbox**, or Serato on a controller, remain the right tool for the job. You need instant key changes, instant BPM matching, the ability to take a request and have it ready in seconds, and zero risk of a needle skip during the first dance. Vinyl is a beautiful format. It is not the format you want carrying the timeline of someone's wedding reception.

Where the vinyl revival does matter for event bookings is in **tone and texture**. If you're planning a cocktail hour, an engagement dinner, or a rooftop pre-party in Mar Mikhael and you want something that feels less like a wedding DJ and more like a curated listening session, that's exactly the mood vinyl-style programming creates — even if it's played digitally off a well-organized crate of 70s and 80s selections rather than physical records. The aesthetic and the pacing are what people are actually responding to, not necessarily the format itself.

## How a DJ builds a "vinyl mindset" set without the risk

This is where experience matters more than gear. After 25 years playing everything from Beirut clubs to Gulf festival stages, the habit that carries over from vinyl days isn't the turntable — it's the discipline of **knowing every track in the set before you touch it**. That means:

1. Pre-listening to every record in the crate, digital or physical, so nothing gets played blind.
2. Sequencing by feel and key, not just by genre tag.
3. Leaving space between tracks instead of rushing the next drop.
4. Committing to a track fully rather than mixing out early because a room hasn't reacted yet.

That discipline is what a good crate digger brings to a modern digital set — the patience and the ear, ported over into Rekordbox or Serato where the reliability is built in. ## Where to hear it in Lebanon right now

Mar Mikhael remains the neighborhood to watch for this — its density of small bars means format experimentation happens there before it reaches bigger Beirut clubs or the Faqra mountain scene. If you're planning a smaller private event — an engagement dinner, a birthday, a pre-wedding gathering — and you want that intimate, record-store energy rather than a full club setup, it's worth asking your DJ directly whether they can program that way. Not every DJ built for big rooms can shrink down to a 40-person bar set and make it feel intentional rather than empty.
