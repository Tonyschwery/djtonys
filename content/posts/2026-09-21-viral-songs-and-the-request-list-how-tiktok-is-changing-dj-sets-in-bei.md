Every few weeks, a new fifteen-second clip takes over everyone's phone, and by the weekend, someone at the request table in Mar Mikhael is asking for it by name. Sometimes it's a genuine hit. Sometimes it's a sound that peaks online and dies the moment the algorithm moves on. Either way, it's changed how I plan a night out — and it's changed what couples expect from a **wedding DJ in Lebanon** too.

## Why viral songs matter more than they used to

Ten years ago, a track built its audience through radio, clubs, and word of mouth. That took months, sometimes years. Now a song can go from unknown to unavoidable in a weekend, purely off a trending audio clip. That speed cuts both ways for anyone booking entertainment for an event in Beirut, Faqra, or the Gulf.

The upside is real: a viral track can create instant recognition on a dance floor. People react to it the way they'd react to a song they've heard a hundred times, even if it only came out last month. That's useful energy, and a good DJ knows how to spend it.

The downside is that virality doesn't mean the track is actually well made for a dance floor. A lot of viral audio is built for a fifteen-second loop, not a full arrangement with a proper build, breakdown, and outro. Some of it is genuinely great music that happened to blow up. A lot of it is a hook with nothing behind it.

## The three questions I ask before a viral track earns a spot

1. **Does it survive past the hook?** If the only good fifteen seconds are the fifteen seconds everyone's already heard, it's not going in a wedding set where I need three or four minutes of usable material.
2. **Does it fit the room I'm actually playing for?** A track trending with sixteen-year-olds on one app isn't automatically right for a mixed-generation reception in Faqra with grandparents on the floor next to the bridal party.
3. **Can I mix it in and out cleanly?** If a track has a weird tempo, an off-grid intro, or no clean edit available, it costs me more than it's worth. I'd rather hold it for the exact right moment than force it into the set.

## Weddings are where this gets tricky

At a Lebanese wedding, the stakes are higher than at a club night. You get one chance to get the timeline right — zaffe, entrance, first dance, dinner, the shift into full dance floor energy. A viral track can absolutely earn its place in that second half of the night, once the floor is loose and the crowd wants recognisable, high-energy material. But dropping something purely because it's trending, at the wrong moment, can flatten a room that was building nicely.

This is where experience matters more than access to trends. I've been doing this for over 25 years, across genres from 70s soul to 90s dance to whatever's blowing up on Instagram Reels this month. The song is never really the point — the room is. A track earns its slot because it moves the specific people in front of me, not because it has a lot of views.

## Mar Mikhael's bars move faster than weddings

Mar Mikhael is a different animal. The bars there run on quick turnover — short attention spans, people moving between venues, a crowd that wants energy fast. A viral track can work brilliantly there because the room doesn't need three minutes of build-up. It needs immediate recognition, and Mar Mikhael's crowd, especially the younger end of it, will react to a trending sound the second it drops. That's exactly the kind of room where a DJ can afford to take that risk — the cost of a track not landing is low, and the reward if it does land is a room that goes up instantly.

## What this means if you're booking a DJ

If you're planning a wedding, private party, or corporate event, here's the honest version of what to ask:

- **Ask how the DJ sources new music, not just how big their old library is.** A 25-year catalogue is worth nothing if it's not paired with a current ear.
- **Ask how they'd handle a specific request for something trending right now.** A good answer isn't "yes" or "no" — it's "depends where we are in the night."
- **Ask whether they mix live off Rekordbox or Serato with a current library, or work from a fixed pre-built playlist.** The first gives you a DJ who can actually read the room and drop the right viral track at the right second. The second gives you a night that sounds the same as everyone else's.

## Building the library to keep up

Behind the scenes, this is a lot of work most guests never see. Keeping a set current means constantly pulling in new tracks, checking they're properly tagged for BPM and key, finding clean edits without the awkward mid-song ad-libs that some viral rips carry over, and testing how a track actually behaves once it's mixed into a set rather than played in isolation on a phone speaker. ## The classics still win

Here's the part that surprises people: even with everything moving faster, the songs that reliably fill a dance floor in Beirut are still mostly the ones that have already proven themselves for years or decades. A viral moment can add a spark, but it rarely replaces a well-placed 90s classic or a disco record that's been working dance floors since before most of the room was born. The trend is a spice, not the meal.
