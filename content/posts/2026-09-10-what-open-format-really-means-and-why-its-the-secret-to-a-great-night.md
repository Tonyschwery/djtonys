People ask me all the time what kind of music I play. My honest answer is: whatever the room needs. That's not a dodge — it's a job description. In the industry, we call it **open format**, and it's one of the most misunderstood terms in the business.

## What does "open format" actually mean?

An open format DJ isn't locked into one genre or era. I might go from a 90s R&B classic into a Fairuz-adjacent Arabic pop moment, into a disco cut from the late 70s, into a house record that's barely a year old — all in the same set, all mixed cleanly, all on purpose.

Compare that to a genre specialist: a **techno resident** who plays a four-hour underground set in a Beirut basement club is doing something different, and doing it brilliantly. That's not open format — that's a curated, singular journey. Both skills matter. But most events, especially in Lebanon, don't call for a niche. They call for range.

A wedding in Faqra with three generations on the dance floor. A private party in Mar Mikhael where half the guests want deep house and the other half want to hear something they can sing along to. A corporate event in Beirut where the crowd shifts from polite to loose over the course of the evening. None of these situations get solved by one genre. They get solved by a DJ who can move between worlds without the transition feeling like whiplash.

## Why genre specialists and open format DJs need each other

I want to be clear about something: open format doesn't mean "plays everything badly." The best open format sets are built by DJs who've spent real time inside each genre they touch — not just skimmed the surface.

That's where 25 years matters. I didn't learn 70s disco, 80s new wave, and 90s house as a checklist. I lived through the labels, the pressings, the radio edits, the club versions. When I drop a **Blackhole Records-era house track** next to something from the Sensual Ibiza catalog, it's not a random jump — there's a harmonic or emotional thread connecting them. That's the craft underneath the "plays a bit of everything" reputation.

## How this plays out at a Lebanese event

Here's roughly how an open format night breaks down in practice:

1. **Arrival and dinner** — low-energy, textured music. Often a blend of chill Arabic instrumentals, soft disco, and lounge cuts. Nobody's dancing yet, and nobody should have to.
2. **First hour of dancing** — this is where open format earns its name. A mix of eras and languages, testing the room, watching what makes people move.
3. **Peak hour** — once you've found the room's center of gravity, you commit to it. This might mean 90s dance classics for an hour straight, or a run of Arabic pop hits, or big-room house — whatever the floor told you it wanted.
4. **The comedown or the second wind** — depending on the crowd, you either bring it back down for last dances, or you push into a final surge before the night ends.

None of that works if you can only play one lane. It also doesn't work if you can play every lane but can't read which one the room actually wants right now.

## Reading the room is the real skill

Genre knowledge is table stakes. **Reading the room** is the actual job. I'm watching who's on the floor, who's at the bar, whether the energy is genuine or performative, whether a request is a real signal or one person's personal agenda. In Beirut, in Mar Mikhael especially, crowds are quick to tell you — with their feet — whether you've made the right call. That immediate feedback is part of what makes playing there so sharp a training ground.

For anyone building their own sense of what open format programming looks like — how tracks get grouped by energy and era rather than just genre — it's worth spending time with well-organized, DJ-ready libraries. Sites like [topdjcrates.com](https://topdjcrates.com) put together curated WAV packs that make it easier to see how tracks across decades and styles can sit next to each other in a set, which is a useful reference point even if you're just a music fan trying to understand how a set gets built.

## What to ask before you book

If you're planning a wedding in Faqra, a private party in Mar Mikhael, or a corporate event in Beirut, and you're not sure whether the DJ you're considering is genuinely open format or just has a long playlist, ask these:

- Can you walk me through how you'd structure the first two hours versus the last two?
- What do you do when a request doesn't fit the moment?
- Which decades or genres do you feel deepest in, and which do you bring in more sparingly?
- Do you build the set live, in the room, or do you plan it in advance?

The answers will tell you a lot faster than any genre list on a website.
