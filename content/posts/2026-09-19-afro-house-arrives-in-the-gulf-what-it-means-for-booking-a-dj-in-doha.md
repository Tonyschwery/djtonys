Walk into a club in Dubai Marina or a rooftop in Mar Mikhael right now and there's a good chance you'll hear it before you can name it: a rolling, percussion-heavy groove somewhere around 118-124 BPM, vocal chants stacked over congas and shakers, a bassline that never quite lets go. Afro house has stopped being a niche import and become one of the defining sounds on dance floors across the region. If you're planning an event this season, it's worth understanding why — and what it means for the DJ you book.

## What afro house actually is

Afro house grew out of South Africa's house scene in the 2010s, built on the foundations of deep house and disco but layered with live percussion, tribal chants, and rhythms drawn from across the continent. Artists like Black Coffee helped carry it onto the world's biggest stages, and it has since splintered into subgenres — Amapiano's log-drum swing, Afro tech's harder edges, melodic Afro house's more emotional, festival-ready builds.

What makes it interesting for a working DJ is how well it sits next to the music Tony has played for 25 years. Afro house shares DNA with 70s disco — the same warmth, the same emphasis on groove over drop. A well-programmed set can move from a classic disco cut into an afro house record without the room noticing the join.

## Why it's landed so hard in the Gulf

**Doha and Dubai have always been fast adopters of global club trends**, and afro house arrived through the same channel as deep house and Balearic sound did a decade ago — international resident DJs, festival lineups, and a crowd that travels and hears the record everywhere before it hits home. Add a climate that suits open-air, sunset-into-night events, and a genre built around slow-building, percussive energy fits the setting perfectly. It works at a beach club at golden hour just as well as it works at 1am indoors.

**Beirut has come to it from a slightly different angle.** Lebanese crowds have always responded to rhythm-forward, vocal-driven music — it's not far from the energy of a good Arabic house set. Afro house's percussion often sits comfortably next to the darbuka and clapping patterns already familiar in Lebanese wedding and club music, which is part of why it's found an audience in Mar Mikhael's bars and on Beirut's bigger club nights without needing much translation.

## What it means for your set list

If you're booking a DJ for a private party, wedding, or club night this year, afro house isn't something to demand track-for-track — it's something to ask about as a texture. A few practical points:

- **Ask how it's used, not just whether it's played.** A good DJ threads afro house into an energy curve rather than dropping a wall of it. It works best as a bridge — after disco and funk classics, before harder house or techno, or as the backbone of a sunset-to-night transition.
- **Check the BPM range fits your event.** Most afro house sits comfortably in the 116-124 BPM range, which overlaps with deep house and disco. That makes it easy to mix in and out of without a jarring tempo jump — useful for weddings where you don't want abrupt energy swings.
- **Ask about vocal content.** A lot of afro house leans on chants and hooks in multiple languages, which can read as fresh and international to a mixed-nationality guest list — a common scenario at Beirut and Gulf weddings with diaspora families.
- **Don't let it dominate a Lebanese wedding set.** It's a great accent, not a replacement for zaffe energy, dabke tempos, or the Arabic and Western staples guests actually came to dance to.

For any DJ taking this seriously, the harder part isn't finding one or two viral afro house records — it's building a properly organised, DJ-ready collection with clean transitions, correct key tags, and consistent mastering so it actually mixes well against everything else in the crate. ## Where this fits at a Lebanese wedding or Gulf event

Tony's approach with afro house mirrors how he treats any trend that shows up strong on the radar: test it against the room before committing to it. A club crowd in Beirut on a Friday night can handle a full afro house segment. A wedding reception in Faqra with three generations on the floor needs it used more sparingly — a track or two to freshen the energy between the classics that actually get everyone up.

That's really the skill underneath all of this. Genres come and go — disco, Balearic, deep house, now afro house — but the job stays the same: **read the room, use the trend where it earns its place, and never let a new sound crowd out the songs your guests actually came to hear.**

## A few questions worth asking any DJ about genre trends

1. Have you actually played afro house live, or just added it to a playlist?
2. How do you decide when a trending genre fits your event versus when it doesn't?
3. Can you show me how you'd transition from something classic — 70s, 80s, or 90s — into a newer genre without losing the room?
4. What's your BPM and key strategy for keeping those transitions smooth?

If a DJ can answer those clearly, they're paying attention to more than just what's charting this month.
